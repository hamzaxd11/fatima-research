# Comparative Analysis of Maternal Education, Knowledge, and Menstrual-Hygiene Practice

## Scope and Evidence Base

The comparison used a structured search of PubMed and OpenAlex, independent DOI Handle and Crossref checks, and locally retained evidence for 12 primary studies. Pakistan was prioritized because the present study was conducted in Lahore. The final set contains five Pakistani studies, six other South Asian studies, and one Ethiopian study included because it reports adjusted maternal-education associations for both knowledge and practice. Eleven studies were checked from complete full text; Yadav et al. was restricted to its verified publisher abstract after the journal's PDF endpoint failed. This is a comparative review, not a meta-analysis.

## Maternal Education and Practice

The present study found a statistically significant unadjusted practice-score difference across maternal-education categories (H = 10.1562, p = 0.0379), with an epsilon-squared effect size of 0.0853. A conservative exploratory Holm sensitivity correction (adjusted p = 0.0758) and restriction to complete practice responses (p = 0.0705) attenuated this evidence. Two external studies reported associations in the same general direction. In rural Varanasi, Kansal et al. reported pad use among 62% of girls with literate mothers and 13% of girls with illiterate mothers. In western Ethiopia, maternal secondary-or-higher education was associated with higher adjusted odds of good practice (AOR 2.03, 95% CI 1.38-2.97).

The evidence is not uniform. Bhusal's Nepal study reported that girls with literate mothers had lower adjusted odds of good practice (AOR 0.52, 95% CI 0.30-0.88), although literate fathers had higher odds. That inverse result should not be dismissed, but neither should it be interpreted as proof that maternal literacy worsens practice. The direction may reflect exposure coding, residual confounding, differences in household roles, or model instability. It shows that maternal education is not a universal stand-alone proxy for supportive menstrual environments.

## Maternal Education and Knowledge

The present omnibus maternal-education comparison was not significant for knowledge (H = 8.0427, p = 0.0900; epsilon-squared = 0.0676). An exploratory ordinal trend test was statistically significant (rho = 0.2023, p = 0.0267), illustrating that the conclusion depends on the selected contrast. Upashe et al. reported that maternal secondary-or-higher education predicted good knowledge (AOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.02-2.22). Direct comparison remains limited because the present study used a bounded score and unadjusted, imbalanced groups, whereas Upashe et al. used a binary outcome and an adjusted model in another setting.

Pakistani studies do not resolve this question directly. Shah et al. reported that 65.3% of mothers had no education and discussed this as a possible contributor to poor knowledge, while Aziz et al. and Michael et al. recorded maternal education but did not model it against outcomes. Those papers support biological and social plausibility but do not estimate a maternal-education effect. The present study therefore adds a direct local test that was largely missing from the Pakistani evidence reviewed.

## Mothers as Information Sources

Across Pakistan, mothers commonly function as the main menstrual-information channel. Michael et al. reported mothers as the main source for 67% of adolescents in Quetta. Aziz et al. found mothers were the dominant source in both urban and rural Khairpur schools, and Shah et al. found mothers were the principal source among girls who had information before menarche. This makes a household pathway plausible, but information-source frequency is not equivalent to information quality. None of these studies showed that maternal education independently caused better knowledge or behavior.

## Knowledge Does Not Guarantee Practice

The present positive knowledge-practice correlation (Spearman rho = 0.3650, p < 0.001) is consistent with the closest Lahore comparator: Afzaal et al. reported r = 0.649 (p < 0.001). Other studies show why this relationship should not be overstated. In Yadav et al., most respondents had fair or good knowledge, but only 40% of girls met the good-practice definition. Dasgupta and Sarkar found that 48.75% knew sanitary pads were appropriate while only 11.25% used them. These studies support the present interpretation that knowledge helps but does not fully determine behavior.

## Structural and Educational Context

Resource constraints provide a plausible explanation for the remaining knowledge-practice gap. Wasan et al. found that only 25% of 25,305 rural Sindh participants used materials classified as appropriate; the poorest wealth group and participants with no education had much higher odds of inappropriate material use. Alam et al. found that accessible girls' toilets were associated with less menstrual-related school absence, whereas activity restrictions were associated with more absence. These cross-sectional findings are consistent with roles for household resources, product availability, privacy, sanitation, and norms, but do not establish their causal effects.

Haque et al. reported large increases in high knowledge and good practice after a six-month school education program. The result supports intervention feasibility but comes from an uncontrolled before-after design, so secular change, repeated measurement, and social-desirability effects cannot be excluded. It justifies testing education and caregiver engagement prospectively rather than claiming that education alone will produce sustained behavior change.

## Overall Interpretation

The strongest defensible conclusion is that the statistically significant unadjusted practice association is compatible with some external studies, while conservative sensitivity analyses indicate that it requires confirmation and should not be interpreted as universal or causal. Maternal education may relate to practical routines through communication, purchasing power, supervision, or household norms, but age differed across education groups and may confound the result. The non-significant omnibus knowledge comparison does not exclude an ordinal trend. The contradictory Nepal result and the absence of direct maternal-education models in most Pakistani studies make replication with balanced groups and multivariable adjustment essential.

The present study contributes locally relevant evidence because it directly compares both knowledge and practice scores across maternal-education levels in a Lahore school. Its small effect size, cross-sectional design, unadjusted analysis, and sparse upper education categories require caution. Future studies should prespecify validated score thresholds, measure product access and school WASH conditions, distinguish maternal education from maternal menstrual-health literacy, and test mediation rather than treating education as a sufficient causal explanation.
