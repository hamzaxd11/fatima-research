# Comparative Evidence Synthesis

The present study included 120 adolescent girls from a school in sub-urban Lahore. Practice scores
differed significantly across maternal-education groups in the primary unadjusted comparison
(Kruskal-Wallis H = 10.1562, p = 0.0379, epsilon-squared = 0.0853), although a conservative
exploratory Holm sensitivity correction (adjusted p = 0.0758) and the complete-response analysis (p
= 0.0705) attenuated this evidence. The omnibus knowledge comparison was not significant (H =
8.0427, p = 0.0900, epsilon-squared = 0.0676). Knowledge and practice were positively correlated in
an exploratory rank analysis (Spearman rho = 0.3650, p < 0.001).

Wasan et al. conducted a cross-sectional baseline assessment among 25,305 participants aged 15-23
years in rural Sindh, Pakistan. Only 25% used an appropriate menstrual hygiene management material,
while 61.9% used old cloth. Compared with higher secondary or university education, no participant
education was associated with inappropriate material use (OR 3.90, 95% CI 3.36-4.52). The exposure
was the participant's education rather than parental education, but the study supports both an
education-practice pathway and the importance of material access. These findings were verified from
the full text and PDF (`../paper_text/wasan2022.txt:4`, `:6`, `:423`; DOI 10.7189/jogh.12.04059).

Aziz et al. studied 318 girls from one urban and one rural government school in Khairpur, Sindh.
Satisfactory knowledge was reported by 69.8% of urban participants and 38.4% of rural participants;
good practice was reported by 71.1% and 11.9%, respectively. Mothers were the main information
source in both groups. Maternal education was recorded descriptively but was not tested against
knowledge or practice. The study therefore provides strong Pakistani evidence of contextual
variation without establishing a maternal-education effect. The findings were verified from the
full-text XML (`../paper_text/aziz2024.txt:35`, `:134`, `:609`, `:617`; DOI
10.1177/17455057241231420).

Shah et al. surveyed 300 girls aged 13-22 years in two institutions in Ghizer, Pakistan. More than
half (51.7%) had not been aware of menstruation before menarche, 47.7% used reusable cloth or
towels, and 30.7% used disposable pads. Mothers were the main information source among participants
who had prior awareness, while 65.3% of mothers had no formal education. Maternal education was not
tested inferentially, so the study supports a maternal information pathway but not a causal or
independent education effect. The findings were verified from the full-text XML
(`../paper_text/shah2023.txt:21`, `:23`, `:27`; DOI 10.3390/ijerph20146424).

Michael et al. conducted a facility-based cross-sectional survey of 923 adolescents in Quetta,
Pakistan. Mothers were the main information source for 67% of participants, 77.7% had received no
menstrual-education session at school, 44.1% identified menstruation as physiological, and 68.7%
used commercial pads. Maternal education was described but not tested against the outcomes. The
study reinforces the importance of maternal communication and shows that incomplete formal knowledge
can coexist with relatively common pad use. The findings were verified from the full text and PDF
(`../paper_text/michael2020.txt:28`, `:155`, `:208`, `:399`; DOI 10.1186/s12905-019-0874-3).

Afzaal et al. studied 108 high-school girls from the rural and semi-urban Lakhodair Sharif Bakra
Mandi area of Lahore. Knowledge and practice were positively correlated (r = 0.649, p < 0.001).
Maternal education was reportedly collected, but no association with the outcomes was presented.
This is the closest geographic comparator and supports the present knowledge-practice relationship
without testing the focal maternal-education association. The findings were verified from the full
text and PDF (`../paper_text/afzaal2024.txt:11`, `:24-27`, `:35`, `:77`; DOI 10.7759/cureus.73899).

Dasgupta and Sarkar conducted a descriptive cross-sectional study among 160 rural secondary-school
girls in India. Although 67.5% knew about menstruation before menarche and 48.75% knew sanitary pads
were appropriate, only 11.25% used pads. Mothers had informed 37.5% of participants. Maternal
literacy was proposed as an explanation but was not tested. The study therefore demonstrates a
knowledge-practice gap and a maternal information role without providing an effect estimate. The
findings were verified from the full-text XML (`../paper_text/dasgupta2008.txt:10`, `:103`; DOI
10.4103/0970-0218.40872).

Kumar et al. used a mixed-method community cross-sectional design among 650 rural girls in Varanasi,
India, of whom 590 had attained menarche. Premenarcheal awareness was reported by 29.4%, and 31%
used sanitary pads. Pad use was greater among girls with literate mothers than among girls with
illiterate mothers (62% versus 13%), and the association was statistically significant; the
regression direction also favored maternal literacy. This finding agrees with the direction of the
present practice result, although hygiene was defined largely through absorbent use and the exposure
categories differed. The findings were verified from the full-text XML
(`../paper_text/kansal2016.txt:6`, `:69-71`; DOI 10.4103/0970-0218.170964).

Yadav et al. conducted a cross-sectional study among 276 grade 7-8 students in Nepal, with practice
assessed among 141 girls. Fair knowledge was reported by 67.4% and good knowledge by 26.4%, but only
40% of girls met the study definition of good practice. This result supports separate assessment of
knowledge and enacted practice. The locally available evidence was limited to the publisher
abstract, so parental-education analysis could not be assessed (`../paper_text/yadav2018.txt:1`; DOI
10.3126/jnhrc.v15i3.18842).

Bhusal conducted a multistage cross-sectional study among 406 girls in ten schools in Nepal; 67.0%
met the study's good-practice threshold. In the results table and abstract, girls with literate
mothers had lower adjusted odds of good practice (AOR 0.52, 95% CI 0.30-0.89), whereas girls with
literate fathers had higher odds (AOR 2.55, 95% CI 1.26-5.15). These estimates contradict the
expected direction for maternal education and the present pattern. The article's narrative paragraph
reports slightly different confidence limits and a different paternal estimate, but the abstract and
results table agree with each other and were therefore used here. The findings were verified from
the full-text XML (`../paper_text/bhusal2020.txt:7`, `:33`, `:173-194`; DOI 10.1155/2020/1292070).

Haque et al. evaluated an uncontrolled school intervention among 416 girls aged 11-16 years in
Bangladesh. After six months, high knowledge increased from 51.0% to 82.4% and good practice from
28.8% to 88.9%. Maternal education was recorded but not tested as an effect modifier. The findings
show that education can coincide with improved knowledge and practice, although the absence of a
control group prevents causal attribution. The findings were verified from the full-text XML
(`../paper_text/haque2014.txt:14-16`, `:236`; DOI 10.1136/bmjopen-2013-004607).

Alam et al. analyzed a nationally representative cross-sectional survey of 2,332 girls in 700
schools in Bangladesh. Menstrual-related absence was reported by 41%. Access to girls' toilets was
associated with less absence (adjusted prevalence difference -5.4 percentage points, 95% CI -10.0 to
-1.6), whereas menstrual restrictions were associated with greater absence (adjusted prevalence
difference 9.1, 95% CI 3.3-14.0). No maternal-education effect was estimated. The study shows that
facilities and social restrictions can shape behavior independently of knowledge or maternal
education. The findings were verified from the full-text XML (`../paper_text/alam2017.txt:4-6`,
`:384`; DOI 10.1136/bmjopen-2016-015508).

Upashe et al. conducted a school-based cross-sectional study among 828 high-school girls in
Ethiopia. Good knowledge was reported by 60.9% and good practice by 39.9%. Maternal secondary or
higher education predicted good knowledge (AOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.02-2.22) and good practice (AOR 2.03,
95% CI 1.38-2.97). This adjusted result agrees with the direction of the present practice finding
but differs by also identifying a knowledge association. Differences in thresholds and setting limit
direct comparison. The findings were verified from the full text and PDF
(`../paper_text/upashe2015.txt:6`, `:210`, `:290`; DOI 10.1186/s12905-015-0245-7).

The studies were not pooled because their instruments, populations, and definitions of adequate
knowledge or practice differed. The present continuous score means were not converted into good or
poor categories without validated cutoffs. Cross-sectional associations were interpreted as
associations rather than causal effects. Pakistani evidence was given the greatest contextual
relevance, while findings from India, Nepal, Bangladesh, and Ethiopia were used to assess whether
the observed patterns recurred across settings. The contradictory Bhusal result was retained because
the relationship between maternal education and menstrual hygiene was not uniform.
